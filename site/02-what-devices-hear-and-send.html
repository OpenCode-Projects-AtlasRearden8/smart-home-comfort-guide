This is the chapter that makes everything else make sense. We'll walk through each of the four device types and answer two honest questions: what can it hear? and what does it send out of your home? No scare tactics — most of this is ordinary and manageable — but it's better to know than to guess.

The four senses, in one table

DeviceWhat it can hearWhat it sends (typically)
Smart speakerSound in the room — voice, TV, music. A hot word (e.g. "Alexa", "Hey Google", "Siri") usually wakes it; most recent models also wake on a wake sound or button pressVoice clips after the wake word (if cloud voice is on), app activity, music habits, usage stats
Smart plugNothing. No microphone at allPower-on schedules, on/off state, sometimes energy readings, to its cloud (unless it's a local-control plug)
Smart thermostatNothing audible (a few premium models have voice/sound features you can skip)Temperature, humidity, occupancy (motion or "someone's home" signals), schedules, heating/cooling runtimes
Home cameraNothing audible unless it has a speaker/mic feature (most have both — more below)What the lens sees — clips or a live stream — plus motion alerts, and usually audio if enabled

A good mental model: speakers and cameras have ears/eyes and want your words and images; plugs and thermostats are mute devices that want your habits — when things turn on, and when your home is occupied.

Smart speakers, plainly

  • The microphone is always powered — that's how it hears the wake word. This is normal and expected, not a fault.
  • What matters is what triggers a recording: the wake word, a wake sound (some models have this, check your settings), or you pressing the button.
  • If your speaker's companion app shows a "Voice History" or "Recordings" page, that is where any saved clip lives — and usually where you can delete them and turn saving off. See the Privacy Settings Table.
  • The mute button on the device (or the physical switch, on most models) cuts the microphone at the hardware level. When muted, most devices show a red light, an orange dot, or a crosshatch on the light ring. There is no cloud pause that beats a physical mute.

Smart plugs, plainly

  • No microphone, no camera. The humblest device here.
  • What it knows is on/off state and (on some models) how much energy it passes through. Where that goes depends on the brand's design: cloud-only plugs send it to the maker's servers; local-control plugs keep the schedule and status inside your house, handled by your hub or the plug itself and shown to you by a local app.
  • If plug data leaving your home would bother you, this is exactly the case where Local Control First pays off.

Smart thermostats, plainly

  • It does not listen, but it does watch patterns: when the house warms, when it cools, when someone seems home.
  • Thermostats are the one device most families are happy to connect — the automation genuinely helps. Comfortable middle ground: keep it connected, but review the schedule and occupancy settings so its "smart" matches your life rather than rediscovering it slowly.
  • Occupancy features ("sensors noticed somebody") can usually be turned off later if the house becomes predictable.

Cameras, plainly

  • Cameras are the one device type where placement is a privacy decision. Lenses pointed at your own front door: normal. Lenses into a living room, a bedroom, or a neighbor's windows: worth reconsidering.
  • Most cameras include a microphone as well, because two-way talk is a selling feature. If you never use two-way talk, look for a setting named "audio recording" or "microphone" and switch it off.
  • Where your clips land matters: cloud subscriptions keep footage on the company's servers; local storage (a microSD card in the camera, or a hub/NVR in your closet) keeps it in your house. See Home Cameras for a plain walk-through.
  • If seeing strangers' property bothers your conscience the same way seeing yours bothers them — that instinct is right. Aim inside your own property line.

The three questions to ask about any gadget

Before (or after) inviting any device into your home, you can ask just three things:

  1. What are its senses? Microphone, camera, presence, power meter?
  2. Where do its memories go? Straight to a company's cloud, to a hub inside your house, or to a memory card in the device itself?
  3. Can it do its job if the internet is out? If yes, you've found a local-capable device — the quiet hero of this manual.

You don't need the perfect answer to question three — "the lights still turn off at bedtime when the internet is down" is a very achievable bar.

Calm summary

  • Speakers: ears always on, recordings only after being called.
  • Plugs and thermostats: no ears, but they remember routines.
  • Cameras: eyes (and ears) — placement and storage settings are your privacy levers.
  • Everything above has an off switch, a mute switch, or a settings page, and the coming chapters show you all of them.
