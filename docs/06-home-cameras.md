# Home Cameras

Cameras are the most useful *and* the most personal of the four device types.
A lens watches what you point it at, so where you point it — and where the
recordings live — is the whole privacy story. This chapter keeps it calm:
what to use one for, where to put it, and how to check the settings that
matter.

## What cameras are genuinely good for

- **Peeking at the porch** — "is that the seed delivery or a raccoon
  delivery?"
- **Watching a sleeping nursery** from another room, with the screen off by
  default.
- **Checking on a pet** mid-shift.
- **Seeing who's at the door** without tromping down the hallway.
- Package and porch peace of mind when you travel.

## Two placements, one rule

The rule: **point at what you own or are responsible for.** Your front porch,
your driveway, the nursery, the dog's crate. Not the neighbor's yard, not
shared hallways in apartments or rented shared spaces, and think twice about
lenses into bedrooms and living rooms you'd feel odd being recorded in
yourself. Inside your own home, many people find one lens aimed at the front
door and one at the nursery is plenty; whole-house fish-eye coverage is
usually more anxiety than it's worth.

## Cloud storage versus local storage (plainly)

When the camera records something, the recording needs to *go* somewhere.
Two paths:

- **Cloud (subscription)**: your device sends clips to the maker's servers.
  Easy to set up, survives a smash of the camera, and typically has a
  monthly fee. The privacy cost is real and literal: your home's moments
  live on someone else's machine.
- **Local storage**: a microSD card inside the camera, or a hub/NVR box that
  stays in your closet. No fee, and history never leaves the house. Costs:
  slightly fiddlier setup, and a stolen camera takes its card's history with
  it (some cameras let you stash history on a hub instead, which softens
  that).

Both paths are reasonable. The calm way to choose: if "my footage only lives
at home" matters to you, pick a camera that has an SD slot or hub support —
that's a single product-page line, and enough to filter the wall of options
fast.

## Microphone settings: the forgotten half

Most cameras include a mic for two-way talk. If you've never used two-way
talk, open the app's camera settings once and look for:

- **"Audio recording"** — switch to off if unused.
- **"Microphone"** / **"Two-way audio"** — same idea.
- **Speaker volume** — reduce if it startles the dog.

This is the camera equivalent of the speaker's mute button, and it's
normally one toggle.

## Motion settings that save sanity

The default motion sensitivity notices every shadow. Fix that once and the
device becomes a pleasant companion instead of a nervous one:

- **Detection zones**: draw a box over the porch, not the street.
- **Person/pet/vehicle detection**, if offered — meaningfully fewer false
  alerts than generic motion.
- **Quiet hours** or scheduled notification silence overnight.
- **Alert cooldowns** ("at most one alert per 2 minutes") if offered.

## First ten minutes, once

1. Mount it where the rule above works, and check the view from the app
   before screwing anything in tight.
2. Set storage (SD card / hub / cloud) deliberately — not by default.
3. Set microphone behavior.
4. Draw detection zones; set quiet hours.
5. Change the default password to something you don't reuse. If the app
   offers two-factor authentication, take it.

## When you're leaving or selling a camera

Cameras are the device you most need the factory-reset skill for: before
selling or giving one away, factory reset it *and* remove it from your
account, so its stored Wi-Fi credentials and any clips are gone. Full steps
per device type are in [Muting and Factory Reset](09-muting-and-factory-reset.md).
